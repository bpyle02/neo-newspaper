A Trump Amnesty Deal Would Betray Not Just His Voters But All Americans

America’s immigration laws are not something the Republican Party or Trump should trade away after a bad election. At a July 2024 rally, then-candidate Donald Trump told a crowd of cheering supporters what he’d do on his first day back in office: “We will begin the largest deportation operation in the history of our country.” Days earlier, delegates at the Republican National Convention waved signs that read “Mass Deportations Now.” Yet in a Sunday interview with CBS News’ 60 Minutes, Trump suggested an amnesty deal may be on the table with Democrats. Reflecting on the loss at the ballot box on Tuesday, Trump told Ross Douthat that his deportation policy might have been “too tough.” “I think it might be too tough, yeah. And a lotta people would say that it’s great. But a lotta people think it’s too tough,” the president said. Trump then pointed to frustration that some Americans have because they believe that illegal aliens working in hotels, landscaping, and other jobs should not have been deported and noted there are others who want illegals who have managed to evade the law for “10 or 15 years” to stay. When asked about working with Democrats on it, he said it was “actually doable.” In short, the president who ran on mass deportations is once again floating amnesty. If his takeaway from Tuesday is that Republicans need to let more illegals stay, somebody ought to show him exit polls. As bad as Tuesday was for Republicans, voters gave Republicans an edge on immigration, with an NBC News exit poll showing that 43 percent of voters said GOP “is better able to handle immigration” compared to 41 percent who said Democrats. Another poll showed that when asked how important immigration policies were in deciding who to vote for, 73 percent of voters said immigration was a “crucial or a major factor” and among those, Republicans won 51 percent to 47 percent. Democrats’ own advertising suggests they knew how poorly a campaign against immigration enforcement would play outside their base. An Axios analysis finding that “ICE” was the third-most featured policy topic in Democrat primary ads for 2026 but that when it came to the general election, it was ranked 35th among policy issues talked about in Democrat ads. But even if the polls showed Trump data that his enforcement policy was a problem to the voters who turned out (Republican turnout was lower than Democrats’, which means oversampling could be likely), Trump still owes Americans the enforcement he promised. Our immigration laws are not something the Republican Party or Trump should trade away after a bad election.

‘Wanted’ review: Bizarre Broadway bandit musical goes off the rails

Much like “Thelma & Louise,” the new female bandit duo musical “Wanted,” which opened Sunday night at the James Earl Jones Theatre, drives off a cliff. At every step, the Broadway show by Ross Baum and Angelica Chéri squanders what’s good about it — namely an enticing premise and a cast of powerful singers — and instead churns out a gaudy, uninvolving slog in which plot movements are at the same time bonkers and blasé. This exhausting and confusing journey through Texas made me blue, all right. Often plays and musicals turn out to be much better than their plain descriptions would suggest. “Maybe Happy Ending” and “Oh, Mary!” come to mind. But “Wanted” is far, far worse. Chéri based the story on two of her ancestors: Mary and Martha Clarke, black sisters who were able to pass as white, and advantageously used that quality to go on a crime spree in the Lone Star State during the late 1800s. That should make for a delicious combination of pieces: a classic Western; R&B, blues and gospel music; and the Harlem Renaissance sensibility of Nella Larsen’s novel “Passing” (which was made into a very good movie starring Tessa Thompson and Ruth Negga). Unfortunately Chéri, Baum and director Stevie Walker-Webb’s efforts instead collide into a stodgy wannabe Frank Wildhorn show, in both aesthetic and attitude, as it garishly, loudly and routinely stomps through history. Thinly drawn Mary (Solea Pfeiffer) and Martha (Liisi LaFontaine) at least have a strong initial thrust to their motivations. Their sharecropper mother Tallulah (Ledisi) is about to lose their home, and the girls set off to legitimately make $200 to save it.
