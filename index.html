By The Past Standards Of The U.S. Military, Nidal Hasan’s Scheduled Execution Is Remarkably Polite

Betrayal, dishonor, cowardly violence, willful cruelty to defenseless victims: rapid and certain execution upon discovery. This is the American military norm. At the beginning of June 1943, American soldiers searching for a missing nine-year-old girl in Algeria found her body face down in a field. An army doctor examined the body and concluded that the child had been sexually assaulted and suffocated, probably several days earlier. A soldier, Pvt. James Kendrick, had been seen with the girl. He told a series of shifting stories that ended with the claim that the sexually aggressive child had come on to him and asked to have sex, after which she suddenly and inexplicably just started to choke. Kendrick’s court-martial opened on June 12, quickly convicting him and sentencing him to death. He was executed by hanging on July 17. Pay close attention to the timeline: crime in very late May, discovery in early June, trial in mid-June, execution in mid-July. Later, the army dug up Kendrick’s body in Algeria and transferred it to a new cemetery plot, Plot E at the Oise-Aisne American Cemetery in France. That hidden plot, locked away behind tall walls, is where the American military relocated the bodies of 96 soldiers executed in Europe and North Africa during World War II. The graves in Plot E are marked only by a number, the names of the dead denied to them in shame. Visitors are rare, and need special permission for brief and supervised entry. The army quickly executed soldiers who shamed their uniform and betrayed their brothers-in-arms, then took the trouble to relocate the bodies to a place where they couldn’t be honored, visited, or mourned. You can find the stories of the army’s 96 hidden bodies in Col. French MacLean’s detailed history of Plot E, The Fifth Field. Those stories tend to go about the same way. Pvt. Fred McMurray and Pvt. Louis Till raped a pregnant woman in Italy, causing her to miscarry, and raped a second pregnant woman, then shot and killed a third woman who resisted as they tried to force their way into her home. The crime took place on June 27, 1944. Administrative delays slowed the opening of the court-martial – because the US Army was kind of busy in Europe in the summer of 1944 – so the executions by hanging weren’t carried out until July 10, 1945. By the prevailing standards, a year was a long wait. Soldiers who murdered a pregnant woman could expect to be executed.

A smartphone can share location and other device data that may reveal sensitive details about where you go. (Tero Vesalainen/Getty Images) Smartphones can use advertising identifiers that help advertisers and developers measure ads and activity. On Apple devices, the system advertising identifier is known as the IDFA. Android devices can use Google's advertising ID. Advertising identifiers can help connect activity across apps when apps and advertising systems are permitted to use them. Combined with location data and other signals, those identifiers can help companies build a much more detailed trail of where a device has been. And a trail stops being anonymous fast. A device that sleeps at your address every night and turns up at a dialysis center every Tuesday and Thursday isn't much of a mystery to anyone who can connect those locations with other identifying information. An app asks for your location. Often for a fair reason, like providing a local forecast or showing the nearest pharmacy. What many people don't realize is that apps can include advertising and analytics software from outside companies. Depending on the app, its permissions and its privacy practices, that technology may collect or receive information about your device and location. Location information can also become part of the digital advertising ecosystem, where information about a device can pass among multiple companies. Once location data leaves your phone, controlling where it goes next becomes much harder.
