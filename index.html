Los Angeles officials appear to have made a damning concession in a filing related to the legal feud over 20,000 people identified as "likely noncitizens" who are registered to vote. In a lawsuit filed Thursday, the Department of Justice demanded that state and L.A. County officials refrain from tabulating the vote-by-mail ballots of the individuals in question, at least for the federal election in less than two weeks. California automatically sends out a mail-in ballot to all registered voters. 'California has not acted on the list of 20,000 suspected noncitizens or agreed to set aside their ballots. American citizens’ votes are being diluted in California.' Earlier this month, California officials denied a similar request from the federal government and accused the government of making difficult demands to cause a political spectacle. On Monday, the DOJ requested an emergency temporary restraining order to force officials to set aside the potentially illegal ballots, but the state's officials are still rebelling. California officials filed motions to oppose the emergency TRO on Tuesday — and soon after, First Assistant United States Attorney for the Central District of California Bill Essayli posted a damning admission to the state's cause. "Massive concession: Los Angeles election officials just told a federal court they have no independent way to verify citizenship beyond what voters attest. (They simply take their word for it)," he posted in a statement on social media. "Under federal law, only citizens may vote in federal elections," he added. "Yet California has not acted on the list of 20,000 suspected noncitizens or agreed to set aside their ballots. American citizens’ votes are being diluted in California." Essayli pointed to a section from the motion where a Los Angeles official said officials were not "equipped" to meet the citizenship certification demands of the government in time for the election. "The Registrar is not equipped or authorized to verify voter citizenship because it does not independently house citizenship-related information beyond the voter attestation as identified in federal and state law (such as, among other things, immigration records, travel records, naturalization records, etc.) and certainly is not equipped to do so within the election period," the defendants wrote. "California adheres to Federal Law passed by Congress in the 1990s [see 52 U.S.C. 20504(c)(2) (Section 5 of the NVRA)]. May want to brush up on your election law before tweeting. Ever notice that websites keep asking you to verify your identity when you're using a VPN? A VPN can trigger repeated security checks when websites detect unfamiliar or shared IP addresses. A dedicated IP may help reduce login interruptions. (Photo by Firdous Nazir/NurPhoto via Getty Images) First, let's set the record straight: A VPN generally does not interfere with how your passkeys or two-factor authentication codes work. Time-based authenticator codes are generated on your device and can work without an internet connection. Passkeys use cryptographic keys to verify your identity during a sign-in, while push notification approvals require a network connection. A VPN can usually work alongside all of these methods. What it does change is the public IP address websites see. When you connect through a VPN server, websites and apps see that server's IP address instead of your usual public IP address. That's the whole point. But it's also why banks, email providers, and financial apps sometimes get nervous. These services use your IP address as one of many signals to decide whether a login looks legitimate. If you always log into your bank from roughly the same location and the same address, and suddenly your account is being accessed from a different city or a different country, the system flags it. Not because your VPN broke anything, but because from the app’s point of view, someone new just walked in the door.
